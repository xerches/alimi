## 1. Purpose

Encryption turns a lost laptop into an inconvenience instead of a breach notification, and keeps data private on its way across the internet. This policy says where encryption is required, which methods are acceptable, and how the keys that make it work are protected. Weak key handling undoes strong encryption, so most of the policy is about keys.

## 2. Scope

All {{Company}} devices, systems, cloud services and applications that store or transmit Internal, Confidential or Restricted information; all cryptographic keys, certificates, secrets and tokens; and software {{Company}} builds, under the Secure Development and Change Management Policy.

## 3. Roles and responsibilities

| Role | Responsibilities |
| --- | --- |
| {{Security Lead}} | Owns the policy, approves algorithms and key management arrangements, keeps the key and certificate inventory. |
| {{IT Lead}} or managed service provider | Enforces device encryption, manages certificates and the secret manager, and monitors expiry. |
| Developers and system owners | Use approved libraries and services, never roll their own cryptography, and keep secrets out of code. |

## 4. Policy statements

### 4.1 Where encryption is required

1. **Laptops, desktops and phones**: full-disk or device encryption is on before issue (BitLocker, FileVault, or the mobile platform's encryption) and enforced by device management. Recovery keys are escrowed in the management tool, never kept by the user alone.
2. **Data at rest in the cloud**: Confidential and Restricted data is stored only in services that encrypt at rest by default. Customer-managed keys are used where a contract or regulation requires it.
3. **Data in transit**: all connections carrying company data use TLS 1.2 or higher (TLS 1.3 preferred) with certificates from a trusted authority. Plain HTTP, FTP, Telnet and unencrypted SMTP without TLS are not used. Email in transit uses opportunistic TLS at minimum; Restricted data in email uses end-to-end encryption or a secure link.
4. **Removable media and backups**: encrypted, with keys held separately from the media.
5. **Databases and files holding Restricted data**: encrypted at rest, and the most sensitive fields (card data, health identifiers, credentials) encrypted or tokenised at the field level where the system allows.
6. **Passwords** are never stored reversibly; systems {{Company}} builds hash them with a slow, salted algorithm designed for passwords (such as Argon2id or bcrypt).

### 4.2 Approved methods

7. Approved as of this version: AES with 128-bit keys or longer (256 preferred) for symmetric encryption; RSA with 2048-bit keys or longer or elliptic-curve keys of 256 bits or longer for asymmetric use; SHA-256 or stronger for hashing; TLS 1.2 or 1.3 with modern cipher suites. Anything listed as broken or deprecated by NIST or the platform vendor (MD5, SHA-1 for signatures, DES, 3DES, RC4, SSL, TLS 1.0 and 1.1) is not used.
8. {{Security Lead}} updates the approved list when standards bodies or vendors change their guidance, including the move to post-quantum algorithms as platforms make them available.
9. Software uses the platform's or a well-maintained library's cryptography; nobody designs or implements their own algorithm or protocol.

### 4.3 Key management

10. {{Security Lead}} keeps an inventory of cryptographic keys, certificates and secrets that matter: what it protects, where it is stored, the owner, the creation date, the rotation or expiry date, and who can access it.
11. **Generation**: keys are generated by the platform's key service, hardware security module or an approved library with a secure random source, at the approved strength.
12. **Storage**: keys and secrets live in a key management service, a secret manager or the company password manager, with access limited by role and logged. They are never stored in source code, configuration files in repositories, tickets, chat, email or documents. Secret scanning on repositories enforces this.
13. **Use**: a key is used for one purpose (encryption, signing or authentication), not several.
14. **Rotation**: certificates are renewed before expiry (automated where possible); API keys and service credentials rotate at least yearly and immediately when a person with access leaves or a compromise is suspected; encryption keys for data at rest rotate on the schedule the platform recommends or at least every two years.
15. **Backup and recovery**: keys needed to read backups or encrypted archives are backed up separately from the data, so a disaster does not lose both. Recovery procedures are tested with the restore tests in the Backup and Recovery Policy.
16. **Compromise**: a suspected key compromise is a security incident. The key is revoked and replaced, data it protected is assessed, and the Incident Response Policy applies.
17. **Destruction**: keys are destroyed when no longer needed, after the data they protect has been destroyed or re-encrypted, and the destruction is recorded. Cryptographic erase (destroying the key) is an accepted way to dispose of encrypted media.
18. **Separation**: no single person holds the only copy of a key that protects Restricted data or backups; the break-glass arrangement in the Access Control Policy applies.

### 4.4 Certificates

19. Public-facing services use certificates from a trusted certificate authority, with automated renewal where possible. Self-signed certificates are used only inside test environments.
20. Expiry is monitored and alerts go to {{IT Lead}} at least 30 days before a certificate expires.
21. Private keys for certificates are generated on the system that will use them or in a key service, never emailed, and are protected like other keys.

### 4.5 Integrity and signing

22. Where it matters that data has not been changed (software releases, backups, financial exports, health records), integrity is protected with signatures or keyed hashes, and verified before use.

### 4.6 Legal

23. **[HR or legal review]** Export controls and local law can limit encryption in some countries where staff travel. Counsel confirms the countries that need special handling before travel with encrypted devices.

## 5. Exceptions

Follow the Information Security Policy. A system that cannot meet the approved methods (an old device, a legacy protocol) is an exception with a compensating control (network isolation, additional access control) and a replacement date.

## 6. Enforcement

Unencrypted devices are blocked from company services by device management until encrypted. Secrets found in code are rotated and the commit remediated. Deliberate breaches follow the Human Resources Security Policy.

## 7. Review

{{Security Lead}} reviews this policy annually and when guidance on algorithms changes.
