# Verify Before You Pay: 20-minute training video script

**Audience:** everyone at {{Company}} who can create a vendor, change bank details, approve or release a payment, or change payroll. Managers and {{CEO}} included.

**Format:** twelve slides, screen recording with voice, 20 minutes. Timings are targets; a live delivery with discussion runs about 35 minutes. Slides use the brand colours and plain type; no hooded figures, no padlocks, no red warning triangles. Where a slide says "pause", the presenter stops the recording or the room for the time shown.

**Production notes:** record in one take per slide and edit together. Show the poster and the procedure on screen when they are mentioned. Use the composite scenario on slide 3 as written; it is built from public FBI descriptions and is not a client story. Do not add real victim names or amounts from news stories without checking the source.

---

## Slide 1. Title (0:00 to 0:45)

**On slide:** Verify before you pay. {{Company}} payment verification training. Presented by {{Finance Lead}}.

**Say:** Welcome. This is twenty minutes about one habit: before money goes somewhere new, we pick up the phone. By the end you will know the three rules in our Payment Verification Procedure, why each one exists, what to say on a callback, and what to do in the first hour if something has already gone wrong. Nothing here needs technical knowledge. The people this protects against are counting on busy, helpful staff, and the defence is a short phone call.

**Notes:** keep the opening warm. This is not a blame session.

## Slide 2. The crime, in one sentence (0:45 to 2:30)

**On slide:** Business email compromise (BEC): a criminal uses a real or look-alike email account to redirect a payment. FBI IC3 2025 report (published April 2026): 24,768 BEC complaints, $3.04 billion reported lost. Real estate fraud: 12,368 complaints, $275.1 million.

**Say:** The FBI calls it business email compromise, BEC for short. A criminal either breaks into a real email account, often a supplier's, or sets up an address that looks the same at a glance. They wait for a payment to be due, then send a message that changes where it goes. In its 2025 Internet Crime Report, published in April 2026, the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center counted 24,768 complaints and more than three billion dollars in reported losses from BEC in a single year, and that is only what people reported. Home closings are a favourite target because the amounts are large and the date is public. The year before, BEC losses were 2.77 billion dollars; the number goes one way.

**Notes:** the figures come from the FBI's annual report; update them each year from ic3.gov and keep the report year on the slide.

## Slide 3. How it actually happens (2:30 to 5:00)

**On slide:** 1. They get in or get close. 2. They watch and wait. 3. They swap the details. 4. They add a reason to hurry.

**Say:** Here is a composite of what the FBI describes, not a real client. A supplier's bookkeeper clicks a link and types her password into a fake login page. For six weeks nothing happens, except that every email she sends and receives is copied to someone else. The criminal reads the thread with us about a forty-thousand-dollar invoice. The day before it is due, a message arrives in our accounts payable inbox, from her real address, in her usual tone, with the invoice attached, saying the company has changed banks and here are the new details. A rule in her mailbox hides our reply. We pay. Two weeks later the real bookkeeper asks where the money is. The bank tells us the receiving account was emptied the day the wire landed.

Notice what did not happen. Nobody at our company was hacked. The email was genuine. The attachment was genuine. The only false thing was the account number, and the only thing that would have caught it was a call.

**Notes:** slow down on the last paragraph. It is the point of the whole session.

## Slide 4. Why we can't just "spot the fake" (5:00 to 6:30)

**On slide:** Look-alike domains: rn vs m, 1 vs l, company-inc vs companyinc. Real accounts get compromised too. Checking the address is necessary but never enough.

**Say:** We do look at sender addresses, and the procedure asks you to compare them letter by letter. But two things make that insufficient. First, look-alike domains are cheap and convincing; "r n" next to each other reads as "m" at normal size. Second, as in the story, the address can be completely genuine because the criminal is inside it. So spotting fakes is a useful filter, not a control. The control is independent verification: confirming through a channel the criminal does not control.

## Slide 5. Rule 1: the verified callback (6:30 to 9:00)

**On slide:** Call a number you already had, from the contract, the vendor record or the website you typed in. Reach a person you know. Let them read the details to you. Log it.

**Say:** Rule one. Any request to add or change where money goes is confirmed by calling a number we already had before the request arrived. Not the number in the email. Not the number on the invoice. Not a number the caller offers you. Why? Because if the criminal controls the email, they also control every number in it, and the helpful person who answers will confirm anything.

Let me show you what a good callback sounds like. [Play or act the exchange from Script 1 in the callback scripts: reach the main line, ask for the known contact, ask whether they sent the request, have them read the account number, ask why it changed, state the hold.] Two details matter. You let them read the numbers to you, so you are comparing rather than leading. And you ask why the account changed, because a story that shifts under a second question is a red flag in itself.

If you cannot reach anyone you know, you do not pay. You log the attempt and try tomorrow. A real supplier would rather wait a day.

**Notes:** this is the longest slide. If recording, consider a split screen with the script on one side.

## Slide 6. Rule 2: two people (9:00 to 10:30)

**On slide:** Every bank-detail change, and every payment above {{Threshold}}, needs a second person who has read the callback log entry, not the email.

**Say:** Rule two. One person prepares, a different person approves. The approver reads the verification log, where the caller wrote who they spoke to and on what number, and approves on that, not on a forwarded email. The threshold is {{Threshold}}, and the rule applies to every bank-detail change regardless of amount, because the first payment to a bad account is often small on purpose. Nobody approves their own request. That includes {{Finance Lead}} and it includes {{CEO}}.

## Slide 7. Rule 3: the 24-hour hold (10:30 to 11:30)

**On slide:** New or changed bank details wait one business day after the callback. First payment small where the vendor allows it.

**Say:** Rule three is the safety net under the first two. New or changed details are not paid for twenty-four hours after verification. Criminals need speed: they move money on within hours of it landing, and the pressure to pay today is the tell. A day's delay costs almost nothing and gives a vendor time to notice their own breach, gives us time to notice a second odd message, and gives the approver a calm look at the log. Where the vendor agrees, the first payment is small and confirmed as received before the rest follows.

## Slide 8. The boss rule (11:30 to 13:00)

**On slide:** {{CEO}} will never ask by email, text or chat alone for a payment outside the procedure, for gift cards, or for secrecy. Call them. Refusing is correct.

**Say:** A whole branch of this crime is the fake executive. "I'm in a meeting, I need you to wire this today, keep it between us, I'll explain later." It works because people want to help the boss. So here is the boss rule, and {{CEO}} has agreed to it in writing: they will never ask you by message alone for a payment outside the procedure, for gift cards, or to keep a transfer quiet. If you get such a message, call them on the number you have, or walk over. If it really is them, it still goes through the procedure. If you say no to a message like that, you are doing exactly what the company asked. You will be thanked, not told off.

**Notes:** if {{CEO}} can appear on camera for thirty seconds to say this in their own words, do it. It is the most effective slide in the deck.

## Slide 9. Payroll, customers and our own invoices (13:00 to 14:30)

**On slide:** Direct deposit changes: in the payroll system or in person, confirmed by phone before payday. Our customers: our bank details never change by email, and it says so on every invoice.

**Say:** Two more places this bites. First, payroll. A message that looks like it is from a colleague asks HR to change their direct deposit before Friday. We do not take those by message; the employee changes it in the payroll system with their own login, or comes by with ID, and we confirm by phone before the run. Second, our own customers. Criminals who get into our email will send our customers a change notice in our name. So every invoice and every contract says our details never change by email and gives a number to call. If a customer ever asks you to confirm a change you know nothing about, that is an incident. Tell {{Security Lead}} the same hour.

## Slide 10. Red flags: three messages (14:30 to 17:00)

**On slide:** Three example emails, one at a time. Which is the fraud? (Pause 30 seconds on each.)

**Say:** Let's practise. I'll show three messages. For each, decide whether you would act, and what you would do first.

Message one. From our long-time supplier, usual contact, usual signature: "Please note our new banking details effective immediately, attached. Kindly update before the pending invoice." [Pause.] The giveaway is not the address, which is real. It is the change itself. The answer is Script 1: call the number on file, before anything.

Message two. From {{CEO}}'s name, but a personal-looking address: "Travelling, phone is dying. Need you to process a vendor deposit today, details to follow, keep this confidential until the announcement." [Pause.] Urgency, secrecy, an unfamiliar address, and a request outside the procedure. Call {{CEO}} on their known number. Do not reply.

Message three. From a new customer's accounts team: "Can you confirm your remittance details on the attached form so we can set you up?" [Pause.] This one is probably genuine, and still handled carefully: we send our details through our normal channel, we never fill in a form from an attachment, and we remind them our details never change by email. The point is that the procedure does not slow down honest work; it just adds a call.

**Notes:** answer key: 1 is the fraud pattern to stop; 2 is fraud; 3 is legitimate but shows the customer-side habit. If running live, ask people which flags they spotted before you tell them.

## Slide 11. If money has already gone (17:00 to 18:30)

**On slide:** First hour: 1. Bank fraud line {{Bank Fraud Line}}, ask for a recall. 2. Tell {{Finance Lead}} and {{Security Lead}}. 3. Report at ic3.gov today. Keep everything. Do not reply to the fraudster.

**Say:** If you realise a payment has gone to the wrong place, the first hour decides whether we get any of it back. Call the bank's fraud line immediately and say the words "fraudulent transfer, please recall". Then tell {{Finance Lead}} and {{Security Lead}}; they will open an incident, preserve the emails, check the mailboxes for hidden rules, and tell the insurer within its deadline. Report to the FBI at ic3.gov the same day; for large international wires they have a recall process that only works within seventy-two hours. Do not reply to the fraudster, do not delete anything, and do not spend the afternoon working out whose fault it was. Nobody at this company is in trouble for reporting a mistake fast. People are in trouble for hiding one.

## Slide 12. What we are asking of you (18:30 to 20:00)

**On slide:** Call a number you already had. Two people above {{Threshold}} and on every change. Wait 24 hours. The boss rule. Report in the first hour. Poster at your desk; procedure and scripts in [location]; questions to {{Finance Lead}}.

**Say:** That is the whole thing. Three rules, one boss rule, and a first-hour plan. The poster on your desk has all of it on one page, and the callback scripts give you the exact words. Once a year we will run a drill: a realistic fake request will land in someone's inbox. Nobody will be named. We will talk about what was caught and what was not, and we will fix the procedure, not the person. Thank you for the twenty minutes. The next time a vendor changes their bank details, you will know what to do, and you will have saved the company a very bad week.

**Notes:** end on the poster, full screen, for five seconds.

## Quiz answer key and acknowledgement

For the learning management system or a paper sign-off after the video:

1. A supplier emails new bank details from their usual address. What do you do first? **Call the supplier on the number already in our records, not from the email.**
2. Who can approve a bank-detail change alone? **Nobody. Two different people are always involved.**
3. How long do new details wait before the first payment? **One business day (24 hours) after the callback.**
4. {{CEO}} texts you to send a payment today and keep it quiet. What do you do? **Call {{CEO}} on their known number; do not reply to the text; the payment still follows the procedure.**
5. You realise a wire went to a fraudster an hour ago. Who do you call first? **The bank's fraud line, then {{Finance Lead}} and {{Security Lead}}.**
