# Top 10 security needs the market is asking for

2026-10-31 · @Alex

## Bottom line

The world is not short of security products. It is short of people who will take ownership of the basics for a business that cannot hire a security team: make sign-in phishing-proof, govern how staff use AI, vet vendors, patch what attackers actually exploit, and prove all of it to insurers and customers.

Four needs come up in nearly every source: phishing and stolen logins (62% of breaches involve a person), unsanctioned AI use (68% of CISOs see it), third parties (48% of breaches, 55% for small businesses), and unpatched known flaws (now the top way in at 31%). The complaints that nobody is serving well are the "prove it" problems: answering customer security questionnaires, passing cyber insurance underwriting, and writing an AI policy that holds up. Those three match your resume almost exactly.

Consumers are hurting too, with $15.8 billion lost to fraud in 2025, but they pay little for prevention and the market is owned by big brands. Treat consumers as a reputation channel, not a revenue line.

Sources are a mix of independent studies (Verizon DBIR, FTC, ISC2) and vendor surveys (Okta, Mimecast, Hiscox, Torii). Vendor figures are directionally useful but inflate the problem they sell against.

And technical debt and tool sprawl are why "optimizing security tools" is CISOs' third priority, with 36% citing technical debt ([Evanta](https://www.evanta.com/resources/ciso/survey-report/top-3-priorities-for-cisos-in-2026)).

## What consumers are asking for

Consumers want to stop being scammed and to stop fighting their own passwords. Neither is a strong business for a solo founder, because people pay little for prevention and the shelf is crowded.

- **Scams, not hacks.** Imposter scams drew 1.0 million reports and $3.5 billion in losses in 2025. Investment scams cost the most per victim at a $10,400 median. Social media was the costliest contact channel at $2.05 billion, and phone calls had the highest median loss at $1,835 ([FTC data via Experian](https://www.experian.com/blogs/ask-experian/identity-theft-statistics/)).
- **Account takeover is normal now.** About a third of consumers had an account compromised or got a breach notice in the past year, 41% in the US ([FIDO 2026](https://insights.bostonidentity.com/research/fido-2026-05-07-state-of-passkeys-2026-global-consumer-workforce)).
- **Password friction costs sales.** 47% say they would abandon a purchase or sign-in when they can't remember a password. 90% know what passkeys are and 75% have turned them on somewhere, so the fix exists; businesses just haven't deployed it (same FIDO report).
- **AI makes it feel worse.** 87% of Americans are concerned AI is helping scammers, and 51% are more worried than a year earlier ([Pollfish, 1,200 adults](https://www.upwind.io/?p=5420)).

Where this touches your business: your clients' customers feel these problems. Passkeys, DMARC so nobody can spoof the company's email, and a clear scam-reporting path are consumer protections a business buys.

## Where Caprius can fill the gap

Three of the ten needs are underserved and sit squarely on your resume. Build the first offers there.

1. **AI governance for small companies (need #2).** Almost everyone has the problem, few consultants have done it, and you have: AI acceptable use policies, access controls and ISO 42001 work. Sell a fixed-fee "AI policy and inventory" package: find the AI tools in use, write the policy, set the access rules in M365 or Google, train staff. It is also the question now appearing in enterprise questionnaires, which feeds offer 3.
2. **Insurance-ready in 30 days (needs #1, #5, #7).** One package that gets a client to the controls underwriters demand: MFA on everyone, EDR coverage, tested backups, a tabletop-exercised IR plan, and an evidence pack. You cut premiums 15-30% doing this at Boston IT Services, and brokers will refer clients who are failing the questionnaire. The M365 and Google Workspace scanner from your Claude Code plan becomes the diagnostic that opens every engagement.
3. **Questionnaire and trust center service for SaaS (need #6).** Your $1M-unblocked story is the pitch. Build the answer library, stand up a trust center, and own responses on a retainer. This is the one need on the list where a software product could follow, because the answer library is reusable across clients.

Needs #3, #4 and #9 fit as add-ons to a retainer: a vendor review per quarter, a patch SLA report, a SaaS access cleanup at each offboarding. Need #8 is the reason MSPs will subcontract to you.

Skip consumers as a revenue line (#10). A "family security tune-up" could be a goodwill offer for clients' owners and staff, nothing more.

## If you could do everything: does the ranking change?

No. The top 10 measures what buyers complain about, not what you can deliver, so the list stands. What changes is which gaps you can attack directly and how many doors you can open.

Three things shift with unlimited skill:

- **You can own the technical half of the list yourself.** Patching (#4), SaaS and access cleanup (#9) and phishing-resistant sign-in (#1) stop being partner work and become your own high-margin services.
- **You can enter markets that have almost no small-business providers.** Incident response, operational technology, application security and AI agent security are dominated by firms with enterprise minimums. A skilled solo operator who shows up at small-business prices has little competition.
- **The constraint moves from skill to trust and focus.** "We do everything in cyber" is what every MSP website already says, and buyers discount it. Breadth lets you pick any wedge; it does not let you sell all of them at once. Lead with two or three, keep the rest as a menu for existing clients.

One pattern runs through all six: the money is in the referral partner, not the consumer. An estate attorney or wealth manager who can offer a "digital protection" add-on has a reason to send you every client.

## Updated recommendation with the wider scope

Keep the three core offers (AI governance, insurance-ready in 30 days, questionnaires and trust center) and add two wedges that are rare, cheap to deliver and sold through the same referral network of attorneys, CPAs and insurance brokers.

1. **Payment fraud protection.** One product, two markets: the deepfake "verify before you pay" program for finance teams, and the closing-fraud protocol for real estate attorneys and brokerages. Half a day to deliver, almost no competitors, and it opens the door to the insurance-readiness package.
2. **Small-business incident response.** A fixed-fee business-email-compromise response that a 30-person company can afford at 5pm on a Friday. Every incident you handle becomes a retainer client and an insurer relationship, and it builds the forensics muscle an MSSP needs later.

On the consumer side, pick one: the family security concierge, sold to adult children and through elder-law attorneys and wealth managers. Run the abuse-survivor and native-language work as pro bono or sliding scale. It will do more for your reputation in Boston than any ad.

Two things to resist. Operational technology and public-sector work are real gaps, but procurement is slow and the credentials take years; revisit them once the firm has a second person. And do not put the whole offer map on the website. Buyers pay for the specialist who has seen their exact problem, so each niche gets its own page and its own referral partner, while the homepage stays narrow.

## Vanta and Drata: what they charge and where they fall short

Both start around $12,000 a year for a company under 50 people, before the audit. There is no credible compliance product under about $10,000, and that floor is the opening.

|  | Vanta | Drata |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Median annual contract (Vendr deal data) | $20,000, range $7,500 to $57,000, 377 purchases ([Vendr](https://www.vendr.com/marketplace/vanta)) | $24,934, range $9,415 to $68,250, 236 purchases ([Vendr](https://www.vendr.com/marketplace/drata)) |
| Under 50 employees | $12,000 to $28,000 | $12,000 to $28,000 |
| Published list prices | AWS Marketplace: Essentials from $14,000 (1-20 employees), Plus $21,500, Professional $23,000; Trust Center +$6,000; vendor risk +$13,600 ([soc2compliancecost.com](https://soc2compliancecost.com/vanta-cost)) | None published; implementation services $5,000 to $20,000 |
| Typical discount off first quote | About 30% | About 23% |
| Not included | SOC 2 audit ($8,000 to $25,000 for Type II), pentest, consultant to run it | Same |

So a 20-person SaaS company's first SOC 2 year runs roughly $25,000 to $55,000 all in. Both platforms price by framework, headcount band and add-on, and both move you up a tier when headcount, vendor count or questionnaire volume grows.

**What users complain about.** Vanta holds a 4.5 on G2 across 2,892 reviews. The top dislikes by mention count are integration issues (207), pricing (178), expensive (173), limited integrations (172) and missing features (165) ([G2](https://www.g2.com/products/vanta/reviews)). Across G2, Capterra and AWS Marketplace reviews, competitors' roundups add: support that is hard to reach, add-on modules not disclosed during sales, a rigid risk module that teams replace with Excel, noisy alerts, policies generic enough that auditors push back, and an AI assistant that hallucinates answers ([6clicks](https://www.6clicks.com/resources/blog/understanding-vantas-limitations-insights-from-real-user-experiences), [ComplyJet](https://www.complyjet.com/blog/vanta-reviews); both are competitors, so weigh accordingly). Drata draws similar themes at a slightly higher price.

**Where a compliance product could win:**

1. **Below the floor.** A 5 to 30 person company that needs to prove security to an insurer or a customer, not pass a SOC 2 audit, has no product today. Your scanner plus insurer-ready report is exactly that gap, at $500 to $1,500 instead of $14,000.
2. **The decisions the tools do not make.** Vanta and Drata collect evidence. They do not scope the audit, write policies an auditor accepts, or talk to the auditor. Reviewers end up hiring a consultant anyway. A product that comes with the person, at a fixed price, is what they cannot offer.
3. **Outputs for insurers and customers, not auditors.** Everything in these platforms is organized around frameworks. Nobody has built the view a broker or a procurement team actually asks for.
4. **Transparent pricing.** Pricing is their second and third biggest complaint. Publish the price.
5. **Support that answers.** The most repeated qualitative complaint across sources.
6. **AI governance for small teams.** Both support ISO 42001 for customers already paying $20,000. The 30-person company adopting AI gets nothing.

**What not to do.** Do not build a Vanta clone. Their moat is 300+ integrations and auditor relationships, and integrations are also their biggest complaint because they are genuinely hard to maintain. Build adjacent, and join their partner programs: Vanta and Drata both list service partners, and consultants who implement them get referred customers who have already paid for the tool.
